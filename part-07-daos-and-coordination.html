DAOs And Coordination

How decisions actually get made when there's no CEO signing off.

A DAO, a decentralized autonomous organization, is a group that coordinates and allocates resources through code and collective voting, instead of a board and an executive chain of command. The idea is compelling. The execution, in most cases, is messier than the pitch deck ever admits.

01 · WHAT A DAO ACTUALLY REPLACES

Not a company. A coordination layer for one.

A DAO typically holds a shared treasury, funds controlled collectively rather than by one individual, governed through token based or reputation based voting on how those funds get spent. Members submit proposals, the community votes, and if the proposal passes a required quorum, minimum participation threshold, it executes, often automatically through code.

What it actually replaces isn't the entire company. It replaces the trust requirement in one specific function, treasury control. Everything else, the actual building, marketing, and community management, still needs real humans doing real work. A DAO with no active contributors is just a wallet with a voting interface attached.

02 · HOW VOTING POWER ACTUALLY DISTRIBUTES

One token, one vote, sounds fair until you check the wallet list.

Most DAOs weight voting power by token holdings. Sounds democratic. In practice it means whoever holds the most tokens holds the most control, which is closer to a shareholder structure than a democracy. Check the actual token distribution before assuming a DAO is meaningfully decentralized, a handful of wallets controlling majority voting power is common, not rare.

Some DAOs use delegation, letting token holders assign their voting power to an active participant they trust to vote on their behalf, similar to representative democracy. This solves the problem of most holders never actually voting, at the cost of concentrating real influence in a smaller set of delegates.

03 · MULTISIG, WHERE THE THEORY MEETS PRACTICE

The treasury key that needs several signatures, not one.

Between "the DAO decided" and "the treasury moved," there's almost always a multisig wallet, requiring multiple designated signers, often a small trusted group, to approve any transaction before it executes. This exists for good reason, preventing a single compromised key from draining the entire treasury.

This is also where the "decentralized" label gets stretched thinnest. A DAO whose entire treasury moves on the approval of five known signers is decentralized in governance discussion, centralized in actual execution. Neither framing is wrong. Know which one you're evaluating.

04 · WHERE DAOS ACTUALLY BREAK

Coordination is hard. Code doesn't fix that.

Low voter turnout is close to universal, most token holders never vote, which means quorum requirements often get met by a small, motivated minority, not broad consensus. Governance attacks, where an actor accumulates enough tokens specifically to push through a proposal that benefits them at the treasury's expense, are a documented, recurring failure mode, not a hypothetical.

None of this means DAOs are a failed idea. It means the coordination problem they're solving is genuinely hard, and code enforces the voting mechanism without fixing the human incentive problems underneath it. Evaluate a DAO on its actual voter participation and treasury history, not its governance token's price.

A DAO is only as decentralized as its weakest concentration of power. Go find it before you trust it.
