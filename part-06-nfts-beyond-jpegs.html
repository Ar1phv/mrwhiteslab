NFTs Beyond JPEGs

Ownership, provenance, and the soulbound assets that can't be bought at all.

Say NFT and most people picture an overpriced cartoon avatar from 2021. Fair reaction to a real bubble. But the underlying mechanic, a unique, verifiable entry of ownership on a public ledger, has uses far past profile pictures. This part separates the mechanic from the mania.

01 · THE MECHANIC UNDER THE HYPE

Non fungible means one of one, not one of a million.

An NFT is a token where each unit is unique and non interchangeable, unlike the fungible tokens from Part 01 where every unit is identical. That uniqueness is what makes it useful as a record of ownership over a specific thing, a specific artwork, a specific ticket, a specific claim.

The image people associate with NFTs usually isn't stored on chain at all, it's typically hosted elsewhere, referenced by a link inside the token's metadata. What's actually on chain, immutably, is the ownership record and that reference. Understand this and you understand why so many "rug pulled" NFT projects could vanish the artwork while the token itself remained technically intact.

02 · PROVENANCE, THE PART THAT ACTUALLY MATTERS

A complete, unforgeable ownership history.

Provenance is the full chain of custody for an asset, every wallet that has ever owned it, in order, permanently recorded and publicly checkable. In traditional art and collectibles markets, provenance is expensive to verify and easy to fake. On chain, it's free to verify and effectively impossible to fake.

This is the actual utility, not the picture, the picture is just the wrapper. Ticketing, luxury goods authentication, real estate title records, anything where "prove this is genuinely yours, and only yours" has value, is a candidate for this mechanic. Most of the industry hasn't built this yet. The mechanic is ready before the market is.

03 · ROYALTIES AND CREATOR ECONOMICS

Getting paid every time your work resells. In theory.

NFT standards allow creators to encode a royalty, a percentage automatically routed back to them on every secondary sale, forever, without needing a lawyer or a licensing deal. This was genuinely novel for creators historically locked out of resale value on their own work.

In practice, royalty enforcement has been a running fight. Some marketplaces made royalties optional to compete on lower fees, and creators lost income as a result. The lesson is not that the mechanic failed, it's that a mechanic only works if the platforms around it actually enforce it. Check whether royalty enforcement is on chain and unavoidable, or just a polite request a marketplace can ignore.

04 · SOULBOUND TOKENS, OWNERSHIP THAT CAN'T BE SOLD

The most interesting NFT is the one you can never sell.

A soulbound token is non transferable by design, permanently bound to the wallet it was issued to. This flips the entire assumption behind NFTs as speculative assets. It's not built to trade. It's built to represent something that shouldn't be for sale in the first place, a credential, a reputation record, proof of participation, proof of contribution.

This is the exact design behind MARK Protocol, the creator support system I built on Injective, following the EIP-5192 standard. Reputation you can buy isn't reputation. Making it non transferable was the entire point, not a limitation.

Think identity, credentials, and access rights, not collectibles. A university degree as a soulbound token can't be counterfeited or bought secondhand. A contributor badge means something specific precisely because nobody could simply purchase their way into it.

05 · READING AN NFT MARKET WITHOUT GETTING PLAYED

Floor price is a headline, not a fact.

A collection's floor price, the cheapest listed item, gets quoted as if it represents the whole market's health. It usually doesn't. Thin liquidity means a handful of low listings can crater a headline number while actual trading volume tells a completely different story. Check volume and unique buyer count before trusting a floor price alone.

Wash trading, artificially inflating volume by trading an asset back and forth between wallets you control, is common enough in this market that raw volume numbers deserve the same skepticism as raw APY numbers from Part 05. Same discipline, different asset class.

The picture is marketing. The provenance record is the product.
