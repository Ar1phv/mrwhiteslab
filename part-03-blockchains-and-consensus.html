Blockchains
And Consensus

What actually makes one chain different from another, stripped of marketing language.

Every chain claims to be faster, cheaper, more decentralized. Most of that is positioning. Underneath the positioning sits a small set of real engineering tradeoffs. Understand those tradeoffs and you can evaluate any chain in five minutes, without needing a single marketing slide.

01 · WHAT A BLOCKCHAIN ACTUALLY IS

A ledger everyone keeps, and nobody can quietly edit.

Strip away the branding and a blockchain is a list of transactions grouped into blocks, each block cryptographically linked to the one before it. Change one transaction in a past block and the link breaks, visibly, to everyone running the network. That's the entire trick. Not that the data can't be edited, but that editing it is impossible to hide.

The harder problem was never the data structure. It was getting thousands of computers, run by strangers who don't trust each other, to agree on which version of that list is the real one. That agreement problem is consensus, and it's what actually separates blockchains from each other.

02 · PROOF OF WORK VS PROOF OF STAKE

Pay with electricity, or pay with capital at risk.

Proof of Work has miners compete to solve a computational puzzle. Winner adds the next block, earns the reward. Security comes from the sheer cost of the hardware and electricity required to attempt an attack. Bitcoin still runs this model. It's battle tested and brutally simple, at the cost of enormous energy use.

Proof of Stake replaces computation with capital. Validators lock up the network's native asset as collateral. Propose or validate a block dishonestly, and that stake gets destroyed, a process called slashing. Security comes from the cost of the capital at risk, not the cost of electricity. Ethereum moved to this model in 2022. Solana, Injective, and Cardano all run variations of it from the start.

Proof of Work

Security through energy cost. Simple, proven, slow to evolve, expensive to run at scale.

Proof of Stake

Security through capital at risk. Faster, cheaper to run, more design flexibility, newer track record.

03 · FINALITY, THE PROMISE BEHIND "CONFIRMED"

When can you actually trust that it's done.

Finality is the point where a transaction becomes practically impossible to reverse. It matters more than almost anything else when you're moving real size, because it defines when you can actually treat a settlement as final.

Bitcoin has probabilistic finality. Each additional block mined on top of yours makes reversal exponentially harder, but never mathematically impossible, which is why exchanges wait for multiple confirmations before crediting a large deposit. Many Proof of Stake chains, Injective among them, target fast or instant finality, where a block is final within seconds of being produced, backed by a supermajority of validator signatures rather than an accumulating probability.

This is not a minor technical detail. It's why some chains feel instant and others make you wait. Check a chain's finality model before you build anything that depends on knowing, for certain, that a transaction cannot be undone.

04 · THE TRILEMMA

Decentralization, security, scale. Pick your tradeoff.

The blockchain trilemma is the observation that it's extremely difficult to maximize all three of these properties at once. Push hard on scale, throughput climbs, but usually at the cost of running fewer, more powerful validators, which erodes decentralization. Push hard on decentralization, thousands of independent validators, but coordinating them slows the network down.

DDecentralization: how many independent parties secure it
SSecurity: cost to attack or corrupt the network
TThroughput: transactions the network can process

Every chain you'll ever evaluate has made a choice about where it sits on this triangle, whether the team admits it or not. When a project claims to have "solved" the trilemma outright, that's your signal to look closer, not to relax.

05 · LAYER 1 VS LAYER 2

Base settlement, and the traffic built on top of it.

A Layer 1 is a chain with its own independent consensus and validators, Ethereum, Solana, Injective, Bitcoin. It settles and secures transactions itself, from the ground up.

A Layer 2 processes transactions off the base chain, then submits a compressed proof or batch back to the Layer 1 for final settlement, inheriting its security without inheriting its congestion. Rollups are the dominant L2 design, bundling thousands of transactions into one that the base chain verifies. The tradeoff is added complexity and, in some designs, a withdrawal delay while the base chain confirms the batch.

Not every chain needs this split. Some Layer 1s, Solana and Injective among them, are engineered for enough native throughput that they compete directly with rollup performance without needing a second layer at all. Different design philosophy, same underlying goal, more capacity without sacrificing the base layer's security.

06 · WHO ACTUALLY RUNS THESE NETWORKS

Decentralized is a claim. Check the receipts.

Validators, or miners in Proof of Work systems, are the machines that actually produce blocks and enforce the rules. "Decentralized" is a spectrum, not a switch. A network with 20 validators controlling most of the stake is not equally decentralized to one with thousands of independent operators spread across the world.

Before you trust a chain with real value, look at its validator count and stake distribution. Most chains publish this data openly. If they don't make it easy to find, treat that as information in itself.

A chain's marketing tells you its ambition. Its consensus model tells you its actual tradeoffs.
