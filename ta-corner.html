PART A
Candlesticks

The smallest unit on any chart. Everything else in this guide is built from reading these correctly.

01 · ANATOMY OF A SINGLE CANDLE

Four numbers, one shape.

A candlestick shows four prices for one time period, the open, the close, the high, and the low. The thick part, the body, spans open to close. The thin lines above and below, the wicks, mark the high and low the price actually touched, even if it didn't close there.

Color tells you direction. A green or white candle closed higher than it opened, buyers won that period. A red or black candle closed lower than it opened, sellers won it. A long body means a decisive move. A long wick with a small body means the price got pushed hard one way and got rejected back.

Diagram · Anatomy Of A Candle

Green closes above where it opened. Red closes below. The wick shows the full range the price fought through to get there.

02 · SINGLE CANDLE SIGNALS

Three shapes worth knowing on sight.

A doji has a tiny body and wicks on both sides, open and close land almost at the same price. It means the fight between buyers and sellers ended in a near draw, often a sign of indecision after a strong move. A hammer has a small body near the top and a long lower wick, price got sold off hard, then buyers pushed it most of the way back, often read as a bullish signal after a downtrend. A shooting star is the mirror image, a small body near the bottom and a long upper wick, often read as bearish after an uptrend.

Diagram · Doji, Hammer, Shooting Star

Small body, long wick, read by which side the wick sits on and what trend came before it.

A single candle is a clue, never a conclusion. All three of these mean far more when they show up at a real support or resistance level from Part B than when they appear in the middle of nowhere.

03 · TWO CANDLE SIGNALS

When the second candle completely swallows the first.

A bullish engulfing pattern is a small red candle followed by a larger green candle whose body fully covers the red one, buyers didn't just stop the decline, they erased it in one move. A bearish engulfing is the mirror image, a small green candle followed by a larger red one that swallows it whole.

Diagram · Bullish Engulfing

The small red body sits entirely inside the range of the larger green body that follows it. That containment is what makes it a real engulfing pattern, not just two candles in a row.

The same rule applies in reverse for bearish engulfing, at a resistance level, after an uptrend. Neither version means much in isolation, the same rule from single candles applies here too, check where on the chart it happened before trusting it.

PART B
Structure

The levels and lines that give the candles above a context to actually mean something.

04 · SUPPORT AND RESISTANCE

The floor and the ceiling, until they aren't.

Support is a price level where buying pressure has repeatedly stopped a decline. Resistance is the opposite, a level where selling pressure has repeatedly capped a rally. Neither is a hard rule, they're zones where the crowd's memory of past reactions tends to repeat itself.

The single most useful fact about these levels: when resistance finally breaks, it frequently becomes the new support, and when support breaks, it frequently becomes the new resistance. Watch for that flip.

Diagram · Price Respecting A Range

Price tests the same two zones repeatedly. Each touch that holds reinforces the level, each break redraws the range.

Round numbers act as informal support and resistance too, a token sitting at 0.98 dollars will often behave differently right around the 1.00 dollar mark, simply because more people have a mental order sitting there, not because of anything structural in the market itself.

05 · TREND LINES AND CHANNELS

Connecting the dots the market already drew.

A trend line connects a series of rising lows in an uptrend, or falling highs in a downtrend. A channel adds a second, parallel line on the opposite side, framing the range price has been moving inside.

Diagram · Ascending Channel

Each low sits higher than the last. Two parallel lines frame the channel price is climbing inside.

Trend lines are drawn after the fact, from data that already happened. Two people can draw the same chart's trend line slightly differently and both have a reasonable case. Treat a trend line as a zone of interest, not a precise, guaranteed number.

06 · FIBONACCI RETRACEMENT

A pullback rarely retraces to a random number.

After a strong move, price often pulls back before continuing, and those pullbacks tend to stall near a specific set of ratios derived from the Fibonacci number sequence, most commonly 23.6, 38.2, 50, 61.8, and 78.6 percent of the prior move. Draw the tool from the start of a move to its end, and those horizontal levels mark zones traders widely watch for the pullback to find support.

Diagram · Fibonacci Retracement Levels

Price rallies from 100 to 0, pulls back, and stalls right around the 38.2 to 50 zone before continuing higher.

Fibonacci levels work because enough traders watch them and act near them, a self reinforcing effect, not a law of the universe. Combine them with the support and resistance zones from the previous section, a Fibonacci level that lines up with an existing support zone is a stronger signal than either alone.

PART C
Chart Patterns

Shapes that repeat because crowd behavior repeats. Reversal patterns signal an ending, continuation patterns signal a pause.

07 · HEAD AND SHOULDERS

Three peaks signaling an uptrend is running out of energy.

Three peaks, a higher middle peak flanked by two lower ones, roughly equal in height. The line connecting the two lows between them is the neckline. A confirmed close below that neckline is read as a signal the uptrend has exhausted itself. Flip the whole shape upside down and you get an inverse head and shoulders, the same logic applied to a downtrend running out of sellers.

Diagram · Head And Shoulders

Three peaks, the middle one highest. A close below the neckline is the confirmation, not the two shoulders alone.

08 · DOUBLE AND TRIPLE TOPS

Resistance holding twice is a stronger signal than once.

A double top is two roughly equal peaks with a pullback between them, signaling resistance held twice and buyers are losing conviction. A double bottom is the mirror image at a low, support holding twice. A triple top or triple bottom is the same idea a third time, generally read as an even stronger signal simply because the level has now been defended three separate times.

Diagram · Double Top

Two peaks at nearly the same price. The pullback low between them, once broken, confirms the pattern.

Every pattern in this section fails a meaningful percentage of the time. Treat a pattern as raising or lowering probability, never as a certainty you'd bet your entire position on alone.

09 · TRIANGLES

Compression before a decision.

A symmetrical triangle forms when price makes lower highs and higher lows at the same time, compressing into a point, with no strong lean toward either breakout direction. An ascending triangle has a flat top and rising lows, generally read as bullish, buyers are stepping in earlier each time. A descending triangle is the mirror image, a flat bottom and falling highs, generally read as bearish.

Diagram · Symmetrical Triangle

Range compresses toward a single point. The eventual breakout direction, not the triangle itself, is the actual signal.

10 · FLAGS, PENNANTS, AND WEDGES

A pause, not necessarily an ending.

A flag is a small, tight, roughly parallel channel that slopes gently against the prior trend, typically following a sharp move, a brief consolidation before the original direction resumes. A pennant is the same idea but the channel converges into a small triangle instead of staying parallel. A wedge looks similar to a triangle but both lines slope in the same direction, and unlike a triangle it's more often read as a reversal signal rather than a continuation one, the opposite of what its similar shape might suggest.

All three are read the same underlying way, a pause in momentum, confirmed or denied by which direction price actually breaks out of the shape, never assumed in advance.

PART D
Indicators

Math run on top of price, built to make momentum, trend, and volatility easier to see at a glance.

11 · MOVING AVERAGES

Smoothing out the noise to see the direction.

A moving average plots the average price over a set number of past periods. A simple moving average, SMA, weighs every period equally. An exponential moving average, EMA, weighs recent periods more heavily, reacting faster to new price action at the cost of more noise.

When a shorter average crosses above a longer one, it's a golden cross, widely read as bullish. The reverse is a death cross, read as bearish. Both are lagging signals, they confirm a shift that's already underway, they don't predict it in advance. Moving averages also act as dynamic support and resistance, price often bounces off a widely watched average like the 200 period line the same way it bounces off a fixed horizontal level.

Diagram · Golden Cross

The faster average crosses above the slower one. Confirmation of a shift already in progress, not an early prediction of one.

12 · MACD

The distance between two moving averages, turned into its own indicator.

The MACD, moving average convergence divergence, plots the gap between two exponential moving averages as its own line, along with a signal line, a smoothed average of that gap, and a histogram showing the distance between the two. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, it's read as bullish momentum building. When the histogram bars shrink even while price keeps rising, it's an early warning that momentum is fading before price itself turns.

Diagram · MACD Crossover And Histogram

Histogram bars grow as the MACD line pulls away from the signal line, and shrink as they converge, often before the crossover itself happens.

13 · RSI AND DIVERGENCE

Measuring how stretched a move already is.

The Relative Strength Index, RSI, measures the speed and size of recent price moves on a scale from zero to a hundred. Readings above 70 are typically read as overbought. Readings below 30 are read as oversold.

Diagram · RSI Scale

Overbought and oversold are conditions, not automatic signals. A strong trend can stay overbought far longer than feels reasonable.

The more advanced read is divergence. If price makes a new high but RSI makes a lower high than its previous peak, momentum is quietly weakening even while price still looks strong, often an early warning sign ahead of a reversal. Bullish divergence is the mirror image, price makes a lower low while RSI makes a higher low.

Diagram · Bearish Divergence

Price climbs to a new peak while the RSI line beneath it fails to. The chart looks strong. The momentum underneath it says otherwise.

14 · BOLLINGER BANDS

A volatility ruler wrapped around the price.

Bollinger Bands plot a moving average in the middle with two bands above and below it, set a fixed statistical distance away. When the bands squeeze tight together, volatility is low and often about to expand. When they spread wide apart, volatility is high and often about to contract. Price touching the upper band isn't automatically a sell signal, in a strong trend price can ride along the upper band for an extended stretch, the same caution that applies to RSI above.

Diagram · Bollinger Bands

Price weaves between the bands. A squeeze near the middle line often precedes a sharp expansion in either direction.

15 · VOLUME, THE CONFIRMATION LAYER

A move without volume is a move without conviction.

Volume measures how much was actually traded during a given period, and it's the check that separates a real breakout from a fake one. A resistance break on heavy volume suggests real conviction behind the move. The same break on thin volume is far more likely to fail and reverse, a false move, not a real one. A rising price on falling volume is itself a mild warning sign, fewer participants are actually backing the move higher.

Every pattern in Parts A through D gets more reliable when volume confirms it, and less reliable when volume contradicts it. Make checking volume a habit before acting on any pattern above, it's the fastest filter for separating a real signal from noise.

PART E
Execution And Risk

Everything above this line is analysis. This is the part that decides whether being right actually pays you.

16 · TIMEFRAMES AND CONFLUENCE

The same chart tells a different story depending on the zoom level.

A token can be in a clear uptrend on the weekly chart, a sideways range on the daily chart, and a sharp downtrend on the fifteen minute chart, all at the exact same moment, all technically correct at their own zoom level. Confluence is checking multiple timeframes together and trading in the direction where they broadly agree, rather than reacting to whichever single timeframe you happen to be staring at.

A common approach, use a higher timeframe to decide the overall direction you're willing to trade, then drop to a lower timeframe only to time the actual entry within that direction.

17 · ORDER TYPES

How your decision actually becomes a position.

MARKET ORDER

Executes immediately at the best available price. Fast, guaranteed to fill, no control over the exact price you get, especially in a thin book from Part 05.

LIMIT ORDER

Executes only at your chosen price or better. Full price control, no guarantee it fills at all if price never reaches it.

STOP-LOSS ORDER

Sits inactive until price hits a trigger, then becomes a market or limit order to exit a losing position automatically.

TAKE-PROFIT ORDER

The mirror image of a stop-loss, automatically closing a winning position once it hits your target.

A stop-loss and a take-profit set before you enter a trade are what turn a plan into something that survives contact with your own emotions once the position is actually open and moving. Set both before you click buy, not after.

18 · RISK TO REWARD

You can be wrong most of the time and still profit.

The risk to reward ratio compares how much you stand to lose against how much you stand to gain on a given trade. A trade risking one dollar to make three has a 1 to 3 ratio, and at that ratio you can be wrong more than half the time and still come out ahead, a fact most new traders underweight compared to how much they obsess over win rate alone.

Diagram · Risk To Reward Setup

Distance from entry to stop is the risk. Distance from entry to target is the reward. Measure both before you enter, not after.

Break Even Win Rate At A Given Ratio Required win rate = risk ÷ (risk + reward)
At 1 to 3, break even sits at 25 percent. At 1 to 1, break even sits at 50 percent.
19 · POSITION SIZING

The single most underrated skill on this entire page.

Position sizing decides how much of your account a single trade can actually risk, set as a rule before you ever open a chart, not decided in the moment based on how confident a trade feels. A common baseline, risk no more than 1 to 2 percent of total account size on any single trade, so a string of losses, which will happen to everyone eventually, doesn't put the whole account in danger at once.

Position Size Formula Position size = (account size × risk percent) ÷ (entry price − stop price)
Example: a 10,000 dollar account risking 1 percent on a trade with a 2 percent stop distance sizes to a 5,000 dollar position.

This single habit, sized correctly before entry, does more for long term survival than any pattern or indicator covered in Parts A through D. A perfect pattern read with a reckless position size still ends badly. A mediocre pattern read with disciplined sizing survives to trade another day.

PART F
Psychology

Everything above this line is learnable in weeks. This is the part that takes years, and actually decides the outcome.

20 · TRADING PSYCHOLOGY

You can know every pattern in this guide and still lose consistently.

What actually separates traders who survive from traders who don't is almost never chart reading skill, it's discipline under real financial pressure, and that takes far longer to build than any pattern in Part C.

→
FOMO. Chasing a move after it's already happened, because watching it run without you feels worse than the risk of entering late. The trade usually turns against you right after you finally give in.
→
Revenge trading. Taking a worse, larger, less thought out trade immediately after a loss, trying to win the money back fast instead of accepting the loss and resetting.
→
Confirmation bias. Only noticing the chart signals that support the position you already want to be in, and ignoring the ones that don't.
→
Overconfidence after a win streak. A few good trades in a row starting to feel like skill instead of what it usually is, normal variance, right before position sizes creep up past what discipline would allow.
→
Moving your own stop loss. Widening a stop after the trade already moved against you, turning a planned, sized loss into an unplanned, unsized one.

The fix for all five isn't a better indicator. It's position sizing decided before you enter, from Part E, and a risk to reward ratio you check honestly before every trade, not after it's already losing. Decide your maximum loss on a trade before you're emotionally inside it, because the version of you that's already in the trade is not the version making good decisions anymore.

The single most useful trading habit isn't a chart skill at all. It's writing down why you entered a trade before you enter it, then checking that reason honestly against what actually happens. Most bad trades break their own stated reason within the first hour, and most traders never go back to check.

21 · THE TRADE JOURNAL

The habit that turns experience into actual improvement.

A trade journal is a simple record kept for every trade, entry reason, entry price, stop, target, position size, and the outcome. Without one, memory quietly rewrites itself, wins get remembered vividly and losses get minimized or forgotten, which means the same mistakes repeat for years without ever being visible as a pattern.

Review it honestly on a fixed schedule, weekly is common, looking specifically for repeated mistakes rather than just tallying wins and losses. A trader who journals badly and reviews it honestly improves faster than a trader with a perfect strategy and no record of how it's actually being executed.

A chart tells you what probably happened before. Your discipline decides what happens to your account after.
