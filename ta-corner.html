01 · READING A SINGLE CANDLESTICK

Four numbers, one shape.

A candlestick shows four prices for one time period, the open, the close, the high, and the low. The thick part, the body, spans open to close. The thin lines above and below, the wicks, mark the high and low the price actually touched during that period, even if it didn't close there.

Color tells you direction. A green or white candle closed higher than it opened, buyers won that period. A red or black candle closed lower than it opened, sellers won it. A long body means a decisive move. A long wick with a small body means the price got pushed hard one way and got rejected back.

Diagram · Anatomy Of A Candle

Green closes above where it opened. Red closes below. The wick shows the full range the price fought through to get there.

02 · SUPPORT AND RESISTANCE

The floor and the ceiling, until they aren't.

Support is a price level where buying pressure has repeatedly stopped a decline. Resistance is the opposite, a level where selling pressure has repeatedly capped a rally. Neither is a hard rule, they're zones where the crowd's memory of past reactions tends to repeat itself, nothing more mystical than that.

The most important thing to know about these levels: when resistance finally breaks, it frequently becomes the new support, and when support breaks, it frequently becomes the new resistance. Watch for that flip, it's one of the more reliable patterns in this entire section.

Diagram · Price Respecting A Range

Price tests the same two zones repeatedly. Each touch that holds reinforces the level, each break redraws the range.

03 · TREND LINES AND CHANNELS

Connecting the dots the market already drew.

A trend line connects a series of rising lows in an uptrend, or falling highs in a downtrend, giving you a visual read on the market's direction and a rough level to watch if that direction breaks. A channel adds a second, parallel line on the opposite side, framing the range price has been moving inside.

Trend lines are drawn after the fact, from data that already happened, which means two people can draw the same chart's trend line slightly differently and both have a reasonable case. Treat a trend line as a zone of interest, not a precise, guaranteed number.

Diagram · Ascending Channel

Each low sits higher than the last. Two parallel lines frame the channel price is climbing inside.

04 · REVERSAL PATTERNS

Shapes that signal a trend is running out of energy.

The head and shoulders pattern is one of the most cited reversal signals, three peaks, a higher middle peak flanked by two lower ones, roughly equal in height. The line connecting the two lows between them is the neckline. A confirmed break below that neckline is read as a signal the uptrend has exhausted itself.

Diagram · Head And Shoulders

Three peaks, the middle one highest. A close below the neckline is the confirmation, not the two shoulders alone.

A double top is simpler, two roughly equal peaks with a pullback between them, signaling resistance held twice and buyers are losing conviction. A double bottom is the mirror image at a low, support holding twice. Both need a confirmed break of the middle level to actually count, two peaks alone are not yet a pattern, just two peaks.

Every pattern in this section fails a meaningful percentage of the time. Treat a pattern as raising or lowering probability, never as a certainty you'd bet your entire position on alone.

05 · CONTINUATION PATTERNS

A pause, not necessarily an ending.

Where reversal patterns suggest a trend is ending, continuation patterns suggest it's just catching its breath before continuing. A triangle forms when price makes lower highs and higher lows at the same time, compressing into a point, often resolving with a sharp move in the direction of the trend that came before it.

A flag is a small, tight, roughly parallel channel that slopes gently against the prior trend, typically following a sharp move, a brief consolidation before the original direction resumes. Both patterns are read the same way, a pause in momentum, not proof the trend is actually over.

06 · MOVING AVERAGES

Smoothing out the noise to see the direction.

A moving average plots the average price over a set number of past periods, smoothing out the candle by candle noise so the underlying direction is easier to see. A short moving average, like 20 periods, reacts quickly to recent price. A longer one, like 200 periods, moves slowly and reflects the bigger picture trend.

When a shorter average crosses above a longer one, it's called a golden cross, widely read as a bullish signal. The reverse, a shorter average crossing below a longer one, is a death cross, read as bearish. Both are lagging signals, they confirm a shift that's already underway, they don't predict it in advance.

Diagram · Golden Cross

The faster average crosses above the slower one. Confirmation of a shift already in progress, not an early prediction of one.

07 · RSI AND MOMENTUM

Measuring how stretched a move already is.

The Relative Strength Index, RSI, measures the speed and size of recent price moves on a scale from zero to a hundred. Readings above 70 are typically read as overbought, the move has been strong and fast enough that a pause or pullback becomes more likely. Readings below 30 are read as oversold, the opposite condition.

Diagram · RSI Scale

Overbought and oversold are conditions, not automatic signals. A strong trend can stay overbought far longer than feels reasonable.

The single most common RSI mistake, treating overbought as an automatic sell signal. In a genuinely strong trend, RSI can sit above 70 for a long stretch while price keeps climbing. Combine it with the structure from earlier sections, don't trade it alone.

08 · VOLUME, THE CONFIRMATION LAYER

A move without volume is a move without conviction.

Volume measures how much was actually traded during a given period, and it's the check that separates a real breakout from a fake one. A resistance break on heavy volume suggests real conviction behind the move. The same break on thin volume is far more likely to fail and reverse, a false move, not a real one.

Every pattern in this section gets more reliable when volume confirms it, and less reliable when volume contradicts it. Make checking volume a habit before acting on any pattern above, it's the fastest filter for separating a real signal from noise.

09 · TRADING PSYCHOLOGY, THE PART THAT ACTUALLY DECIDES THE OUTCOME

You can know every pattern in this guide and still lose consistently.

Every section above this one is pattern recognition. Pattern recognition is learnable in weeks. What actually separates traders who survive from traders who don't is almost never chart reading skill, it's discipline under real financial pressure, and that takes far longer to build.

→
FOMO. Chasing a move after it's already happened, because watching it run without you feels worse than the risk of entering late. The trade usually turns against you right after you finally give in.
→
Revenge trading. Taking a worse, larger, less thought out trade immediately after a loss, trying to win the money back fast instead of accepting the loss and resetting.
→
Confirmation bias. Only noticing the chart signals that support the position you already want to be in, and ignoring the ones that don't.
→
Overconfidence after a win streak. A few good trades in a row starting to feel like skill instead of what it usually is, normal variance, right before position sizes creep up past what discipline would allow.

The actual fix for all four isn't a better indicator. It's position sizing decided before you enter, not during, and a risk to reward ratio you check honestly before every trade, not after it's already losing. Decide your maximum loss on a trade before you're emotionally inside it, because the version of you that's already in the trade is not the version making good decisions anymore.

The single most useful trading habit isn't a chart skill at all. It's writing down why you entered a trade before you enter it, then checking that reason honestly against what actually happens. Most bad trades break their own stated reason within the first hour, and most traders never go back to check.

A chart tells you what probably happened before. Your discipline decides what happens to your account after.
