Privacy Tech

Mixers, privacy coins, and the regulatory tension that follows this category everywhere it goes.

Part 03 established that most chains are fully transparent by default, every transaction, every balance, publicly visible forever. Privacy tech exists to change that, deliberately. It's also the single most regulated, most contested corner of this entire industry. Understand both sides before you touch it.

01 · TRANSPARENT BY DEFAULT, ON PURPOSE

Public ledgers were the whole design choice in Part 03.

Bitcoin and Ethereum are pseudonymous, not anonymous. Every wallet address and every transaction is permanently visible to anyone who looks, forever. Your name isn't attached automatically, but the moment your identity connects to an address, through an exchange KYC record from Part 14 or a public statement, the entire transaction history behind that address becomes attributable to you, retroactively, with no way to undo it.

This transparency is what makes the consensus model from Part 03 work, everyone can verify everyone else's balance without trusting a central party. It's also a real privacy tradeoff most newcomers don't fully register until it's too late to matter.

02 · MIXERS, BREAKING THE ON CHAIN TRAIL

Pooling funds to obscure where they actually came from.

A mixer pools deposits from many users together, then allows withdrawals to new addresses in a way designed to break the direct on chain link between deposit and withdrawal. Tornado Cash, built on Ethereum, is the most well known example, using zero knowledge proofs from Part 12's vault to let a user prove they made a valid deposit without revealing which specific deposit was theirs.

Tornado Cash was sanctioned by the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control in 2022, after North Korean state hackers used it to launder stolen funds at scale, a first for a piece of autonomous software rather than an individual or company. The sanctions were later challenged in court and partially struck down in 2025, but the episode remains the clearest real world case study of privacy tooling colliding directly with financial regulation.

03 · PRIVACY COINS, PRIVACY BUILT INTO THE BASE LAYER

Not an add on. The chain's actual default.

Rather than a tool layered on top of a transparent chain, some networks build privacy directly into the base protocol. Monero uses ring signatures and stealth addresses so that every transaction is private by default, with no transparent option at all. Zcash offers a choice, transparent addresses or shielded pools using zero knowledge proofs, letting users opt into privacy per transaction rather than having it forced network wide.

Monero

Privacy mandatory for every transaction. No transparent option exists at the protocol level.

Zcash

Privacy optional, chosen per transaction. Transparent and shielded addresses coexist on the same chain.

04 · THE REGULATORY TENSION, STATED PLAINLY

Financial privacy is legal. Money laundering isn't. The line is contested.

Regulators globally argue privacy tools primarily serve illicit finance, and the Tornado Cash case gave that argument real weight. Privacy advocates argue financial privacy is a legitimate right, the same right that makes cash transactions private by default, and that treating a neutral tool as guilty because bad actors also used it sets a dangerous precedent. Both positions have genuine, serious arguments behind them, and this fight is actively ongoing in courts and legislatures right now, not settled.

Multiple exchanges delisted privacy coins following regulatory pressure in various jurisdictions, well before any final legal resolution, a practical consequence worth knowing regardless of where you land on the underlying argument.

Privacy tech sits exactly where the permissionless thesis from Part 01 meets the regulated on-ramp reality from Part 14. That collision isn't resolved. Know that going in.
