01 · THE FIRST QUESTION EVERY REGULATOR ASKS

Is this token a security, or isn't it.

In the US, the classification question that decides almost everything else runs through the Howey Test, a decades old legal standard asking whether an asset represents an investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profit derived from the efforts of others. If yes, it's treated as a security, triggering registration and disclosure requirements built for traditional finance, not for a decentralized protocol with no central issuer after launch.

This single question explains an enormous share of the regulatory conflict in this industry. A genuinely decentralized network with no ongoing dependence on a founding team's efforts has a real argument for not being a security. A token still heavily dependent on one team's continued work has a much weaker one. Most tokens sit somewhere in between, and reasonable regulators and reasonable lawyers disagree on where the line actually falls.

02 · THE SAME ACTIVITY, DIFFERENT RULES BY BORDER

There is no single global standard. There are dozens, at once.

USFragmented across the SEC, CFTC, and state regulators, with jurisdiction itself often disputed
EUMiCA, Markets in Crypto-Assets, a unified licensing framework across all member states since 2024
NGNigeria's SEC issued formal digital asset rules in 2022, later tightened around exchange licensing
AEUAE built dedicated crypto free zones with their own regulatory regimes, attracting exchanges directly

The same protocol can be fully compliant in one jurisdiction and operating in a legal gray zone in another, simultaneously, with no contradiction, because the rules genuinely differ. This is why global protocols increasingly geofence certain features by user location, not as an inconvenience, but as a direct response to this exact fragmentation.

03 · AML AND THE TRAVEL RULE

Where the KYC from Part 14 actually leads.

Anti money laundering requirements are why the KYC process from Part 14 exists at exchanges in the first place. The Travel Rule, an international standard, requires regulated financial institutions, exchanges included, to share sender and receiver identity information for transactions above a set threshold, mirroring requirements that have existed in traditional banking for decades.

This is the direct regulatory pressure point behind the privacy tech tension covered in Part 16. A mixer or privacy coin that obscures the sender and receiver makes Travel Rule compliance structurally impossible, which is exactly why regulated exchanges in most jurisdictions have delisted or restricted them, independent of any court ruling on the underlying legality.

04 · WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS FOR YOU, PRACTICALLY

Not legal advice. A checklist for finding real legal advice.

Nothing in this guide is legal advice, and anyone telling you otherwise online, for free, in a Discord server, should be your first red flag, not your source of truth. Regulation changes fast, varies by exact jurisdiction, and the cost of getting it wrong, for a builder especially, is real. If you're building a protocol, launching a token, or running a business around any of this, a real securities lawyer familiar with your specific jurisdiction is not optional overhead, it's the actual cost of doing this correctly.

For individual users, know your own jurisdiction's rules on capital gains treatment for crypto transactions, and keep your own transaction records regardless of what any exchange provides. Regulatory clarity in this industry is trending toward more structure over time, not less, in most major jurisdictions. Build and operate accordingly.

The chain doesn't ask permission. The regulator who oversees the human using it might. Know the difference and plan for both.
