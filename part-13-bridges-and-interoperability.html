Wrapped assets, bridge mechanics, and the single most hacked category in this industry's history.

Part 04 mapped six chains as if they were separate countries. They are. A bridge is what lets value cross the border. It's also, historically, where the biggest thefts in crypto have happened, by a wide margin. Understand the mechanism before you trust it with real size.

01 · WHY CHAINS NEED BRIDGES AT ALL

Every chain is its own island by default.

Part 03 established that each blockchain runs its own independent consensus. That independence is a feature, and it's also the reason no chain can natively see or verify what happened on another one. An asset native to Solana doesn't just exist on Injective, there's no shared ledger between them. A bridge is the infrastructure that manufactures cross chain movement that doesn't naturally exist.

Most bridges work through a lock and mint model. Deposit an asset into a contract on the source chain, it gets locked there, and an equivalent wrapped asset gets minted on the destination chain, a synthetic IOU representing the locked original. Wrapped Bitcoin on Ethereum, WBTC, is the clearest widely used example, Bitcoin locked on one side, a tradeable representation on the other.

02 · EVERY BRIDGE MAKES A TRUST ASSUMPTION

Someone, or something, has to vouch for the lock.

The destination chain has no independent way to verify that the source chain deposit actually happened. It has to trust a reporting mechanism. That mechanism is where bridges diverge sharply in security model, and where nearly every major exploit has originated.

MSMultisig bridges, a small validator set signs off on transfers
LCLight client bridges, cryptographically verify the source chain directly
OPOptimistic bridges, assume validity, allow a challenge window to dispute it

Multisig bridges are fast and cheap to build, and they concentrate risk into however many keys control that multisig, exactly the centralization tradeoff from Part 07 applied to a much larger pool of locked funds. Light client bridges are more trustless, verifying the source chain's actual state cryptographically, and they're considerably more expensive and complex to build correctly.

03 · THE HISTORY YOU NEED TO KNOW BEFORE YOU BRIDGE ANYTHING

The receipts, not the theory.

The Ronin Bridge exploit, March 2022, roughly 625 million dollars, a validator set compromised through five of nine signing keys falling to a single attacker, a direct multisig trust assumption failure. The Wormhole bridge, February 2022, roughly 325 million dollars, a signature verification flaw in the smart contract itself. The Nomad bridge, August 2022, roughly 190 million dollars, a single misconfigured line of code that let anyone spoof a valid message and drain funds, copied by hundreds of opportunistic wallets within hours once the flaw became public.

Three different failure modes, three of the largest thefts in this industry's history, all bridges. This isn't a reason to avoid cross chain activity entirely. It's a reason to treat bridging as the highest risk single action in your entire on chain workflow, deserving more scrutiny than a swap or a stake, not less.

04 · NATIVE INTEROPERABILITY, THE ALTERNATIVE APPROACH

Some ecosystems are trying to skip the bridge entirely.

Rather than bolting a bridge onto separate chains after the fact, some ecosystems build interoperability into the base protocol. The Inter-Blockchain Communication protocol, IBC, used across the Cosmos ecosystem, lets chains verify each other directly through light clients as a native feature, not a third party add on. Injective's own interoperability, and its Native EVM support referenced in Part 04, leans on this model rather than a bolted on bridge for a large share of its cross ecosystem activity.

This doesn't eliminate risk, no design does, but it removes an entire category of it, the third party bridge operator as a single point of failure. Watch which direction the ecosystems you use are actually building toward.

05 · BEFORE YOU BRIDGE, CHECK THIS

The same discipline from Part 10, applied here specifically.

Check the bridge's security model directly, multisig, light client, or optimistic, and how many independent parties actually control it. Check whether it's been audited, and by whom, the same audit scrutiny from Part 10 applies with extra weight here given the concentration of funds bridges typically hold. Check its track record, a bridge with years of uncontested operation and meaningful volume has earned more trust than a new one promising faster speeds.

For large amounts, consider splitting the transfer, or using a bridge with a proven track record even if it's slower or costs more than a newer alternative. The fee difference is never worth the tail risk on a security model you haven't actually checked.

A bridge is a promise that two separate systems agree on what happened. Verify who's actually making that promise before you trust it with size.
