01 · RESTAKING

Your staked collateral, working a second job.

Restaking lets an asset already staked to secure one network, Ethereum's base layer for example, also be pledged to secure additional protocols simultaneously, earning extra yield in exchange for extra slashing risk. It multiplies capital efficiency and multiplies systemic risk in the same motion, a failure in one restaked protocol can now threaten the validator's stake, and by extension the base chain's security, in a way that didn't exist before this primitive.

Start here: EigenLayer's documentation, and any independent analysis of its slashing conditions and shared security assumptions.

02 · INTENTS

Stating what you want, not how to get it.

An intent based architecture flips the standard transaction model. Instead of specifying the exact execution path, swap on this pool, at this price, through this route, you declare the outcome you want, and a network of solvers competes to find and execute the best path to deliver it. This shifts complexity off the user and onto specialized infrastructure, at the cost of introducing a new trust layer, the solvers themselves, and new questions about how fairly they compete.

Start here: how UniswapX and CoW Protocol implement solver competition, and where MEV protection actually sits in each design.

03 · ZERO KNOWLEDGE PROOFS

Proving something is true without revealing why.

A zero knowledge proof lets one party prove a statement is true to another party without revealing any information beyond the statement's validity itself. Applied to blockchains, this powers zk rollups, batching thousands of transactions and proving their correctness with a single, compact proof the base chain can verify quickly, and privacy preserving applications that let you prove eligibility, solvency, or identity without exposing the underlying data.

Start here: the difference between zk rollups and optimistic rollups, and why proof generation time is still the primary bottleneck most teams are racing to solve.

04 · ACCOUNT ABSTRACTION

Making wallets programmable, not just custodial.

Account abstraction turns a wallet from a simple key holder into a programmable smart contract account, enabling social recovery without a single seed phrase as the only failsafe, spending limits, gasless transactions paid by a third party, and multi factor authorization schemes that don't currently exist in a standard wallet. This directly addresses the seed phrase single point of failure from Part 02, at the cost of added contract complexity and new attack surfaces specific to each implementation.

Start here: Ethereum's ERC-4337 standard, and how it differs from account abstraction implementations native to chains like Starknet.

05 · TOKENOMICS DESIGN

Incentive structures that hold up, versus ones that don't.

Real tokenomics design means modeling supply emissions, vesting schedules, and holder incentives against realistic demand scenarios, not against the most optimistic case in the deck. Study vesting cliffs specifically, a large unlock hitting the market with no corresponding demand growth is one of the most predictable, recurring price events in this industry, and it's disclosed publicly in every reputable project's documentation before it happens.

Start here: any project's token unlock schedule, cross referenced directly against on chain data showing actual circulating supply versus fully diluted supply.

06 · GOVERNANCE ATTACK VECTORS

When voting power becomes the exploit itself.

Building on Part 07, a governance attack exploits the voting mechanism directly, accumulating enough tokens, often through a flash loan for the duration of a single vote, to push through a malicious proposal before anyone can react, then reversing the position immediately after. Defenses include time delays between a proposal passing and its execution, giving the community a window to react, and snapshot based voting that uses historical token balances instead of balances at the moment of the vote.

Start here: the Beanstalk Farms governance exploit, a documented case study in exactly this attack executed in a single transaction.
